H1 Results

Does the Ecological Equation of State predict haul-level community biomass?

Hypothesis 1 asks whether the Ecological Equation of State (EEoS) can predict community biomass at the level of individual trawl hauls in the North Sea International Bottom Trawl Survey (NS-IBTS), quarter 1, 1985–2015. After joining DATRAS length data to FishGlob catch biomass and applying the project’s standard filters, 12,069 hauls entered the analysis. For each haul, three state variables are observed or derived: species richness (S), abundance (N), and normalised metabolic rate (E). EEoS returns a parameter-free biomass prediction, B_pred, from (S, N, E). That prediction is compared with observed catch biomass, B_obs, without any post-hoc calibration fitted to this dataset.

Prior EEoS validations did not ask whether a regression trained on the same data could recover biomass. They asked whether predicted biomass was equivalent to productivity under a direct, unfitted one-to-one map — equivalent to what you get from a one-to-one map with productivity, not to a fit model. H1 follows that same logic. A fitted ln(E) OLS model (log_r2 ≈ 0.60) is reported only as extended context, because it answers a different and easier question.


Test 1 — Absolute biomass prediction against an unfitted 1:1 line

The primary statistic is log_r2, the coefficient of determination on the log scale. In plain terms, this asks how much better the prediction is than simply guessing the average log biomass for every haul. A negative value means the model is worse than predicting the mean: the systematic mistakes are large enough that a constant guess would have done better. This is the metric that penalises absolute scale error. A prediction that is always several times too high can still look orderly on a scatterplot and can still achieve a high correlation, while failing badly on log_r2.

EEoS fails on absolute haul-level biomass prediction. Its log_r2 is −0.22 — worse than guessing the average. The unfitted productivity 1:1 baseline (log(E × m_min) versus log B_obs, using the same normalised E and m_min as the EEoS pipeline) achieves log_r2 of +0.74 on the same footing.

EEoS therefore leaves substantially more unexplained log-scale variance than the simple productivity map. The ratio of residual sums of squares (EEoS SS_res / productivity 1:1 SS_res) is 4.6×. Under the Harte-style criterion that EEoS residual error should be less than half the reference residual error, the criterion is not met. The unfitted productivity map alone outperforms EEoS even though EEoS uses all three state variables through the full partition function. At absolute haul-level biomass scale, the equation of state adds structure that worsens prediction relative to treating calibrated productivity (E × m_min) as a direct proxy.

The EEoS prediction cloud sits systematically above the 1:1 line. The median ratio of predicted to observed biomass is 4.1×. For a typical haul, EEoS predicts about four times more biomass than was caught in the survey haul. Both sides are in grams, so this is not a unit-conversion artefact.


Good ranking, wrong scale

That failure is not “no signal.” Correlation remains high (cor² ≈ 0.93). Squared Pearson correlation of log(B_obs) and log(B_pred) measures rank agreement — whether larger predicted hauls tend to be larger observed hauls — regardless of whether the absolute scale is correct. cor² ≈ 0.93 therefore means ranking is good, but it can hide a ~4× scale miss. There is a correlative relationship between predicted and observed biomass; the problem is systematic offset, not unstructured noise.

Log-scale root mean squared error agrees with the log_r2 ranking. EEoS log RMSE is 1.41; productivity 1:1 log RMSE is 0.65. The productivity map is substantially closer to the observations in absolute log-scale terms.

Null check (brief). A null permutation holds B_pred fixed and randomises B_obs. Observed EEoS log_r2 sits far above the null distributions (one-sided p < 0.001). This does not mean EEoS predicts accurately — log_r2 remains negative. It does mean the haul-specific mapping from (S, N, E) to B_pred carries structure relative to observed biomass. That is a necessary but not sufficient condition for moving to H2/H3, which ask whether residual magnitude tracks disturbance.

The EEoS overprediction is not a fixed multiplicative constant across all hauls. When hauls are split into quartiles of observed biomass, the median predicted/observed ratio increases from the smallest to the largest hauls (roughly 3× in the lowest quartile to over 5× in the highest). If fishing pressure correlates with biomass, a naive constant catchability correction could confound tests that relate residuals to fishing. That is carried forward as a design consideration for H2, not as a claim that H1 has already identified the mechanism.


Test 2 — Harte Figure 2 reproduction (E/B^(3/4) ratio)

Test 2 asks a different question from Test 1. Instead of absolute biomass in grams, it compares the productivity ratio for predicted and observed biomass:

    observed ratio = E / B_obs^(3/4)
    predicted ratio = E / B_pred^(3/4)

using the same B_pred as Test 1 and the same normalised E that enters the biomass function. Following Harte et al.’s plotting code exactly, Pearson R² is computed on the raw (unlogged) ratios — the statistic annotated on their Figure 2 (published reference: R² = 0.600).

Test 1 failure could, in principle, coexist with an approximately constant E:B^(3/4) relationship across hauls, in which case S and N would carry little additional explanatory weight in ratio space. Reproducing Figure 2 checks whether the relative-ratio pattern holds and whether it is comparable in spirit to Harte’s original plots.

NS-IBTS ratios span a much wider range than Harte’s original data, with heavy right skew. Pearson R² on raw ratios is therefore sensitive to a few extreme hauls. High-leverage points are ranked by the product of predicted and observed ratios; Pearson R² is recomputed after excluding the top two. Those hauls are not dropped from the H1 dataset or from Test 1; the diagnostic is scoped to the Test 2 ratio summary only.

Across all hauls, Pearson R² on the raw ratios is 0.964. After excluding the two highest-leverage hauls, Pearson R² is 0.853 — higher than Harte’s 0.600, with broadly the same visual pattern. The trimmed value is the primary number for reporting; the all-hauls value should not be reported alone.

Visually, the NS-IBTS cloud reproduces the qualitative structure of Harte’s Figure 2: a strong association between predicted and observed ratios, but with observed ratios systematically greater than predicted — points lying above the 1:1 line. On the median haul, the observed ratio is roughly three times the predicted ratio (median predicted/observed ≈ 0.35). That is the same directional pattern Harte et al. report for disturbed systems in their original figure: the blue points, which Harte coded as disturbed plots, are the ones that visually show observed ratio exceeding predicted ratio in the same way. The North Sea hauls do not look like Harte’s near-equilibrium validations; they look like the disturbed end of his own Figure 2.

An additional absolute-scale diagnostic on the same ratios — coefficient of determination on the raw ratio values — is much lower (about 0.35). That reinforces the broader pattern already seen in Test 1: strong association or ranking can coexist with weaker absolute-scale performance. Both axes of the Figure 2 comparison share the same per-haul E in the numerator; predicted and observed ratios differ only through the B^(3/4) denominator. A substantial part of the Pearson R² therefore reflects shared-E covariation across hauls. That is also true of Harte’s original Figure 2, which is why Test 2 is best read as a like-for-like parity check rather than as independent proof that S and N explain the ratio. Read Test 2 beside Test 1: even after trimming, relative-ratio agreement remains stronger than absolute biomass-scale performance.


Interpreting H1 in light of disturbance — bridge to H2

Taken together, Tests 1 and 2 do not contradict one another. Test 1 establishes that EEoS does not predict absolute catch biomass at haul scale on NS-IBTS. Test 2 establishes that the relative productivity-ratio structure Harte et al. used to validate EEoS is nevertheless reproduced — and reproduced in the form Harte associated with disturbed, not equilibrium, systems.

That pattern is exactly what would be expected if the North Sea is a fundamentally disturbed system. EEoS is a static equilibrium theory. Under disturbance, a community can deviate from the equilibrium prediction in a systematic direction: predicted biomass too high relative to observed catch (Test 1), and predicted productivity ratio too low relative to observed (Test 2). Prior EEoS work on less disturbed terrestrial and arthropod systems reported equivalence between EEoS biomass and productivity under a direct 1:1 map. The North Sea does not show that equivalence. Instead, its haul-level behaviour aligns with Harte’s disturbed-system plots — not as a failure of replication, but as a clue about why replication “fails” in absolute terms while still matching the ratio figure.

H1 therefore does not end with “EEoS is wrong.” It ends with a more specific claim: EEoS deviance on NS-IBTS is structured, scale-biased, and visually consistent with disturbance as Harte et al. already coded it in their own validation figure. The working explanation developed in supervision is that if disturbance is added as a predictive factor, deviance from the equilibrium prediction should, to some extent, correlate with disturbance. H1 provides the empirical basis for that next step — structured residuals at haul scale, and ratio-space behaviour that matches disturbed systems in the source paper — but does not itself test fishing pressure.

H2 tests that proposition directly. At the scale of ICES statistical rectangles, it asks whether the magnitude of EEoS deviation — mean absolute log residual, mean |log(B_obs) − log(B_pred)| — is associated with fishing pressure (Couce et al. reconstructed trawling hours). H3 extends the same logic temporally. The dependent variable is residual magnitude, not signed residual, because disturbance can push a community either side of the equilibrium prediction; what matters is how far off the equilibrium the system sits, not which direction.


Summary

On North Sea trawl hauls, EEoS does not predict catch biomass on an absolute scale — even though it ranks hauls well (cor² ≈ 0.93) — and under the same fair one-to-one rules used in prior papers, a simple productivity map outperforms the full equation of state by a wide margin (log_r2 +0.74 versus −0.22; SS_res ratio 4.6×). The Harte Figure 2 ratio test, however, is reproduced with Pearson R² above Harte’s published value (0.853 trimmed versus 0.600), and with the same qualitative pattern Harte associated with disturbed systems: observed ratio systematically greater than predicted. H1 failure is therefore read not as unstructured model breakdown but as equilibrium-theory deviance in a disturbed marine system — motivating H2’s test of whether that deviance tracks fishing pressure spatially.
