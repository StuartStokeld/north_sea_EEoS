**Introduction**

Assessing ecosystem condition is fundamental to tracking biodiversity loss, setting restoration targets, and evaluating conservation interventions. The dominant paradigm attempts to quantify condition as relative distance from a reference state, defined as the degree to which an ecosystem's structure, function, and composition deviate from an undisturbed baseline. However, the choice and definition of reference state is critical, as where reference condition is defined as the biotic state in the absence of significant human disturbance (Stoddard et al., 2006; Scholes and Biggs, 2005), systematic spatial bias follows where reference sites are skewed toward remote or protected areas, causing widely-used indicators to overestimate intactness in some of the world's most threatened regions and misdirect conservation prioritisation accordingly (Newbold et al., 2016; Burns et al., 2019). When reference state is estimated based on a fixed point in time (Loh et al., 2005), errors or inaccuracies present in the baseline year propagate into all derived trend estimates (Buckland and Johnston, 2017). The consequences of management based on this have been documented in severe fisheries collapses: Atlantic cod stocks across the Northwest Atlantic were classified as healthy under management reference points anchored to already-depleted baselines, through shifting baseline syndrome (Pauly, 1995; Soga and Gaston, 2018), even as populations crossed irreversible thresholds, contributing to collapses from which recovery has not occurred after decades of moratorium (Turcotte et al., 2025). Dependence on reference state is embedded in every major framework for reporting on nature, across national accounting systems, global risk assessments, carbon and biodiversity credit markets (Czucz et al., 2021; Keith et al., 2013, 2015, 2022; West et al., 2020; Wunder et al., 2025). Recently proposed alternatives, from process-oriented rewilding to best-on-offer contemporary benchmarks, remain comparative, still requiring either a trajectory or a less-disturbed comparator (Perino et al., 2019; Pettorelli et al., 2018; McNellie et al., 2020). These empirical failures point to a deeper conceptual problem, reference conditions are moving targets rather than stable anchors. Where ecosystems have been so altered by human influence that they self-organise into configurations with no historical analogue, references become neither achievable nor ecologically meaningful (Hobbs et al., 2009). Supporters and defenders of ecological integrity concede that a theoretically undisturbed reference condition cannot be known or measured anywhere (Rohwer and Marris, 2021; Karr et al., 2021). Reference-based approaches serve a practical purpose, but where references are unavailable or degraded, outcomes have ranged from systematic underestimation of biodiversity loss to failure to prevent the collapses they were designed to avoid. No ecosystem condition framework exists that is entirely independent of reference populations, historical baselines, or spatial analogues.

Defining a universal measure of ecosystem condition requires a framework that can predict what an undisturbed community should look like from first principles, and detect how disturbance causes systematic deviation from that state. Complex systems theory predicts that disturbance leaves detectable pattern-based signals, including rising variance and critical slowing down, before abrupt collapse (Scheffer et al., 2009; Lenton et al., 2008). In ecological communities, resilience depends on the diversity and redundancy of species performing overlapping functional roles (Biggs et al., 2012), meaning that disturbance which selectively removes functional groups or trophic levels erodes the structural buffer against tipping. Every operational measure of resilience quantifies change relative to a prior state, not an absolute quantity, rendering the problem identical to the reference state problem (Dakos and Kefi, 2022). Macroecology offers a different route: species abundance distributions follow universal ecological laws, changing shape systematically under disturbance before local extinctions occur (McGill et al., 2007; Matthews and Whittaker, 2015). Anthropogenic change drives systematic compositional shifts detectable from community-level pattern data: species richness can remain stable while composition changes rapidly (Dornelas et al., 2014; Magurran et al., 2015). The limitation is that detecting deviation still requires an empirical reference from undisturbed communities, unavailable in chronically exploited systems. Recent theoretical advances offer a resolution to this problem, and have now reached a point of empirical testability. The Maximum Entropy Theory of Ecology (METE) replaces the empirical reference with a theoretical one. Rather than measuring departure from an observed baseline, METE predicts community structure from four aggregate state variables alone, making it generalisable across ecosystems (Harte, 2011). Communities in steady state are predicted to conform to METE predictions; disturbed communities deviate systematically, providing a disturbance signal that requires no knowledge of the pre-disturbance state (Newman et al., 2020). Harte et al. (2022) extended this by deriving the Ecological Equation of State (EEoS), demonstrating that species richness, abundance, and metabolic rate predict community biomass with no free parameters. Williams and Pettorelli (2025) confirmed at continental scale that EEoS prediction error varies with expected ecosystem condition, though satellite-derived proxies establish relative differences across sites rather than absolute accuracy within any site (Harte, 2025). METE in its original form assumes state variables are approximately constant, an assumption that holds in near-equilibrium communities but is violated in dynamically disturbed systems. Harte et al. (2021) addressed this through DynaMETE, which predicts that different disturbance types produce qualitatively distinguishable signatures in community pattern data, meaning EEoS residuals carry information about disturbance type as well as intensity. Collectively these advances offer a potentially vital tool to estimate the condition, resilience and recovery potential of anthropogenically disturbed ecosystems without the need for an observed reference state, but their applicability in ecologically contrasting systems remains largely untested.

For the EEoS to function as a universal condition indicator it must be tested across ecologically contrasting systems. To date, empirical tests have shown generally good performance across undisturbed terrestrial communities (Xiao et al., 2015), but given its proposed use as a condition indicator, the absence of robust tests in disturbed systems is a critical gap (though see Franzman et al., 2021), and the theory has not been tested in any marine fish community. Current marine condition indicators, including the Large Fish Indicator and community mean maximum length, rely on historical trajectories derived from survey data collected under substantial fishing pressure and are therefore vulnerable to the shifting baseline critique (Pauly, 1995; Soga and Gaston, 2018). Despite one of the world's longest-running standardised trawl survey programmes (Maureaud et al., 2024), no study has tested whether EEoS predictions are achievable in North Sea demersal fish communities or whether deviations are systematically associated with fishing pressure. Here, we present the first test of the EEoS in a marine fish community, using haul-level data from the ICES North Sea International Bottom Trawl Survey (Maureaud et al., 2024) combined with spatially explicit fishing pressure data (Couce et al., 2020). We address three hypotheses of increasing specificity. First, we test whether the EEoS predicts observed community biomass from species richness, abundance, and metabolic rate at the haul level better than chance, establishing whether the theoretical baseline is achievable in a marine fish community at all (H1). Second, we test whether deviations from EEoS predictions are systematically larger in areas of high commercial fishing pressure (H2). Third, we test whether individual areas deviate more from the predicted EEoS during periods of higher fishing pressure, and whether temporal change in disturbance within a site produces the within-cell signal that DynaMETE predicts (H3). Together, these hypotheses move from establishing that the EEoS baseline is reachable in this system, through detecting a spatial disturbance signal, to detecting a temporal one, each step increasing the confidence that EEoS residuals carry information about fishing pressure rather than ecological noise.
