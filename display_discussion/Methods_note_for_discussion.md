# Methods note for discussion

## Distinguishing disturbance-driven from sampling-driven deviation from the EEoS baseline

For EEoS residuals to function as a reference-free condition signal, deviation from the theoretical baseline must be attributable to disturbance rather than to properties of the sampling process itself. The aim is to separate ecological signal from noise introduced by how the community happened to be observed (aka "catchability"). Two hauls can depart from the EEoS prediction to the same degree for different reasons. In the first case, the underlying community has been reorganised by disturbance, for example through selective removal of larger or longer-lived taxa under sustained fishing pressure, such that the aggregate state variables (S, N, E) no longer describe a community in the configuration METE assumes. In the second case, the community itself may be close to an undisturbed baseline, but the haul captured an unrepresentative snapshot of it, for instance by intersecting a single-species shoal or cohort rather than a representative cross-section of the local assemblage. Both cases produce an EEoS residual; only the first is informative about disturbance. Any test of H2 or H3 that does not account for this distinction risks conflating fishing-pressure-driven deviation with variance introduced by sampling stochasticity, weakening the interpretability of residual magnitude as a condition signal.

This distinction is grounded directly in METE's own theoretical structure rather than introduced as an external statistical control. METE's central claim is that community structure at steady state converges on the most probable configuration consistent with a given set of state variables, arrived at under the assumption that individuals are distributed across species and body sizes without systematic aggregation (Harte, 2011). A haul in which one species accounts for the overwhelming majority of individuals, particularly where that species is also captured at a near-uniform body size, represents a configuration far from this maximum-entropy expectation, independent of whether the broader community from which the haul was drawn is itself disturbed. Large-scale empirical tests of METE outside marine systems have already identified this specific failure mode: across sixty forest plots, METE reproduced the rank-abundance and individual size distributions closely but performed poorly on the size-density relationship, precisely where taxa depart from the theory's implicit assumption of size-independent, well-mixed placement (Xiao et al. 2015). Schooling and cohort-forming behaviour in pelagic fish species represents a more extreme version of the same violation, and is a feature of sampling rather than of ecosystem condition: whether a trawl intersects a herring shoal on a given occasion is substantially a matter of chance encounter, not a property of the surrounding community's disturbance state.

We therefore treat haul-level compositional typicality as a variable to be controlled for, rather than as a nuisance to be ignored or a disturbance signal to be interpreted directly. Two metrics operationalise this. Numerical dominance (D) is defined as the proportion of a haul's total raised abundance accounted for by its single most numerous species (the Berger-Parker index). Size homogeneity (size_CV) is defined as the coefficient of variation of individual body mass, converted from length via standard length-weight relationships, among individuals of that dominant species within the haul. Both are computed from the same abundance and length data already used to derive the state variables entering the EEoS prediction, avoiding reliance on an external community index disconnected from the theory being tested. Hauls scoring high on D and low on size_CV approximate the single-shoal case described above: numerically dominated by one species captured at a near-uniform size, and correspondingly distant from the well-mixed configuration METE's derivation assumes.

The present analysis applies this as a filter in its simplest form: EEoS performance and the hypothesis tests built upon it are assessed both across the full haul set and after excluding, or conditioning on, hauls at the extreme of the dominance/size-homogeneity distribution, to establish whether the reported relationships are robust to the presence of atypically sampled hauls. This sensitivity approach is preferred at this stage over folding dominance and size-homogeneity directly into the primary hypothesis-testing models. A filter is transparent, avoids introducing model dependencies before the relationship between compositional typicality and fishing pressure itself has been characterised, and keeps a clear separation between the primary test (whether EEoS residuals track fishing pressure) and a secondary robustness check (whether that result depends on the inclusion of atypically sampled hauls). Composite modelling of dominance and size-homogeneity alongside fishing pressure covariates is deferred pending that characterisation, since numerical dominance and size-homogeneity could plausibly correlate with fishing pressure for reasons other than sampling artefact, for example if trawling pressure itself alters community composition toward smaller, more opportunistic, or more aggregation-prone taxa. Establishing whether compositional typicality behaves as an independent nuisance variable or as a partial mediator of the fishing-pressure signal is treated as a necessary step before any such variable is incorporated into the H2/H3 models directly, rather than assumed in advance.

Applied to H1 / provisionally folded into results section pending review:

Hauls whose community composition departs from a well-mixed, evenly-distributed configuration, numerically dominated by one species, or with that dominant species caught at a near-uniform size, show larger overprediction than hauls with a more typical spread across species and sizes. This gradient is smaller than the biomass-magnitude effect, but it persists after conditioning on biomass magnitude, indicating a second, largely independent source of prediction failure. 

Two haul-level metrics were computed to test this: Berger-Parker dominance (D, the share of a haul's total abundance held by its single most numerous species) and a size-homogeneity index (size_CV, the coefficient of variation of body mass within that dominant species' catch in the haul). Both are derived from the same raised abundance and length data already underlying N and E, not from an external diversity index. 

 A nested regression isolates their contribution beyond biomass magnitude alone. A baseline model (ln_ratio ~ log(B_obs)) achieves R² = 0.237. Adding D and size_CV raises R² to 0.245, a partial R² of 0.010: D and size_CV jointly explain about 1% of the residual variance left after biomass magnitude, on top of the 23.7% biomass magnitude explains on its own. Both terms are individually significant and correctly signed (D: β = 0.197, p < 10⁻²²; size_CV: β = −0.055, p < 10⁻³), meaning more numerically dominated hauls overpredict more, and hauls with a more size-heterogeneous dominant catch overpredict less. A D×size_CV interaction term adds nothing (ΔR² < 0.0001, p = 0.79); the two effects act additively rather than modifying one another. 

 This is read as a second, distinguishable mechanism rather than noise: the biomass-magnitude gradient is consistent with a catchability-type scale problem, while the dominance/size-homogeneity effect, small but robust and additive to it, is consistent with a structural departure from METE's entropy-maximising assumption. A haul dominated by one species at a uniform size is, in METE's own terms, close to the theoretical minimum-entropy configuration relative to the well-mixed placement assumption the equation of state is built on, and this is the pattern that assumption predicts about its own failure.

## Other filters that could be applied to tease this out further (if approved by supervisors)

### 5. Local (neighborhood-relative) typicality, rather than global

This one matters more than it might first appear, given what H2/H3 are actually testing. Right now, "atypical" is being judged against the whole 12,069-haul dataset. But a haul that's unusual relative to its own ICES rectangle and season is a different claim from one that's unusual relative to the whole North Sea. If you only ever filter against the global distribution, you risk inadvertently stripping out exactly the disturbance signal H2/H3 is looking for, a rectangle under heavy fishing pressure that's shifted toward a simplified, dominance-heavy community should look atypical globally, and you don't want your sampling-noise filter to quietly remove that. A local version, e.g. Mahalanobis distance of a haul's (S, N, E, D, size_CV) vector relative to other hauls in the same rectangle-season stratum, lets you separate "this haul is weird for its neighbourhood" (more likely a one-off shoal encounter, genuine sampling chance) from "this whole neighbourhood is weird relative to the North Sea" (which is H2/H3's territory, not something you want to filter away). This is probably the most important addition conceptually, since it protects the disturbance signal from being accidentally absorbed into the "control for sampling" step.

### 1. Small-sample noise (a different mechanism from dominance, same goal)

Very low-N or low-S hauls are inherently noisier estimates of local community structure, independent of whether anything ecologically unusual happened. A haul with 15 individuals will look more "extreme" in its SAD purely from sampling variance than one with 1,500, even from an identical underlying community.

**How to apply:** A minimum-N (or minimum-S) threshold, or better, examining whether residual variance scales inversely with N/S as a smooth diagnostic, is a natural complement.

### 2. Undersampling of the rare tail

Proportion of species in the haul represented by singletons (or by fewer than some small count). A haul with a high singleton fraction hasn't necessarily encountered a species-poor community, it may just be a small enough sample that the tail of the local SAD wasn't captured. This is a different failure mode from shoaling: shoaling is "one species massively overrepresented," this is "the rare-species tail is underrepresented," and they needn't co-occur.

### 3. Numeric dominance vs biomass dominance

D is numeric (share of N). Worth also computing a biomass-dominance analogue (share of total B held by the single most massive species, or even the single largest individual). These can diverge in informative ways: a haul dominated numerically by a small abundant species (many sprat) is a different violation from one where nearly all the biomass sits in one or two large individuals (a single big cod carrying most of the metabolic load), even if D looks similar or different in each case. Since EEoS predicts B directly from S, N, E, a single-individual biomass dominance event is arguably a sharper violation of the "well-mixed" assumption than numeric shoaling is.

### 4. Whole-community size structure, not just the dominant species

Your size_CV is currently scoped to the dominant species only. A generalized version: a size-diversity index (e.g. Shannon or Hill-number entropy of the full body-mass distribution across all individuals in the haul, not just the dominant species): would catch cases where the haul isn't numerically dominated by one species but still has an atypical size structure overall (e.g. a haul that's a juvenile recruitment pulse across several species simultaneously).
